Hello everyone. We are presenting our N L P project, “Right Answer, Wrong Reason: Hallucinations in A I Explanations.” Today we'll cover the research problem, the state of the art, our methodology, the proof of concept we've run so far, our preliminary findings, and our next steps.


The question shown with this chart is: “For the M S R C twenty-one dataset, at which query size is the mean absolute error lower without propagation than with propagation?”

Language and vision-language models can answer questions about charts, and they usually explain how they got there. But a correct final answer doesn't guarantee a correct explanation. A model can pick the right bar or the right year while describing a value that isn't in the chart, attaching a label to the wrong bar, or doing arithmetic that doesn't support its own conclusion.

This matters most for scientific charts and numerical data, where we need to trust both the result and the evidence behind it. If we only score the final answer, these errors stay invisible.

So we ask two questions.

Research question one, detection: can we detect hallucinated or unsupported claims in chart explanations, even when the final answer is correct? We'll measure this by comparing an automatic judge against human annotation.

Research question two, reduction: do different prompting and verification strategies reduce these errors? And does more elaborate reasoning actually help, or does it sometimes introduce new errors?

One point on scope. We treat generated explanations and reasoning traces as text to be verified. We make no claim about how the model actually reasons internally.


We reviewed fifteen studies. Three are closest to our work.

Chart Q A X provides about thirty thousand chart question-answer pairs with natural-language explanations. It gives us explanation examples and a generation baseline.

ChartHal studies hallucination in chart understanding with about a thousand human-validated cases, including questions about irrelevant, nonexistent or contradictory chart information. It's a hard benchmark: G P T five reaches only about thirty-four percent accuracy on it.

FaithChart directly asks whether chart explanations are grounded in the visual evidence. Its central message is that a correct answer alone is not sufficient evidence of a well-grounded explanation. That is exactly our motivation.

The remaining work falls into three groups.

Chart benchmarks: Chart Q A, Chart Q A Pro and CharXiv. These mostly evaluate final answers, not explanation quality. On CharXiv reasoning questions, G P T four oh scores forty-seven percent versus eighty percent for humans.

Separating chart reading from computation: DePlot turns charts into tables, TinyChart generates programs, and Faithful Chain-of-Thought uses a deterministic solver.

Reasoning and verification: chain-of-thought, self-consistency, Chain-of-Verification, and Turpin and colleagues' finding that plausible explanations can omit what actually influenced the model.


Our pipeline has three parts.

First, models. We use two small, open-weight multimodal models that run locally: Qwen three point five, nine B, and Gemma four, E four B. We chose open-weight models because they give us full control over versions and settings, which helps reproducibility, and because they return their reasoning traces, so we can check those too.

Second, strategies. We compare exactly four prompting and verification strategies on the same chart questions.

Direct: answer plus a brief explanation.

Structured: first list the relevant chart evidence, then explain.

Chain-of-thought: step-by-step reasoning, with the evidence for each step and the arithmetic shown.

Verification: a second call that re-reads the chart and checks the draft's claims.

Third, annotation. We'll build two evaluation sets. A small, manually annotated set, built from twenty-five chart-question pairs, with two models and four strategies giving two hundred responses. And a larger set of one thousand to ten thousand responses, annotated by a stronger, separate multimodal model acting as an L L M judge.

Annotators label each response for four kinds of errors: incorrect chart readings, invented information, unsupported claims, and calculation errors.

For metrics, we report answer accuracy, the explanation error rate, and the key quantity of this project: the share of correct answers that come with a flawed explanation. We also measure the judge's precision, recall and F one against human labels, plus runtime and token cost.


For the proof of concept, we ran a pilot on five CharXiv validation charts, sampled with a fixed random seed of forty-two.

Each chart went through both models under four strategies: direct, structured, chain-of-thought and two-call verification. That gives forty responses and twenty-five model calls per model. The system prompts are identical across both models.

We then checked each response against the CharXiv reference answer. Separately, we checked the claims inside both the final explanation and the returned reasoning trace. We flagged a response only for a clear, material contradiction with the chart or with its own calculation.

One important caveat. This pilot audit was done by a single reviewer using an L L M, inside a coding environment. It isn't human annotation, and it isn't validated. It's meant to show which error types occur and to shape the larger design, not to give final numbers.


First, answer accuracy. Gemma matched the reference in two of twenty responses, or ten percent. Qwen matched in twelve of twenty, or sixty percent. So fourteen responses had a correct final answer.

Two things stand out. No model answered chart four correctly under any strategy. And verification did not improve the total over direct prompting for either model, even though it doubles the number of calls.

Now the core question. Among those fourteen correct responses, at least three, or twenty-one percent, contained a clearly contradicted claim in the explanation or in the returned reasoning. This is a lower bound, not a final hallucination rate, because a single reviewer checked only for clear contradictions.

By strategy: direct, one of four; structured, one of two; verification, one of four; and chain-of-thought, zero of four. Chain-of-thought had no flagged cases here. However, fourteen correct responses is too little to formulate any conclusions.


Here are the three cases.

The question for this chart is: “Which year does the log hazard rate exhibit the steepest slope for women from age sixty to one hundred?”

One: Gemma with verification, chart one. The final answer, nineteen oh five, was correct. But the reasoning trace said nineteen oh five starts above eighteen eighty-five, when it starts below. It invented starting values, and its own calculation gave equal slopes, which doesn't support either year. It then accepted the draft anyway. This is a reading error plus an inconsistency between answer and reasoning.

Three: Gemma with direct prompting, chart five. The answer, zero point zero six, was correct. But the explanation put the competing line at about zero point three eight when it's around zero point three, exaggerating the gap.

In all three cases, scoring only the final answer would have hidden the problem.


Our pilot shows the phenomenon exists, but five charts can't support firm conclusions. Our next steps are about turning it into a rigorous study.

One: complete the human-annotated set. We'll extend the pilot to twenty-five chart-question pairs, with two models and four strategies, giving two hundred responses. All four team members annotate them.

Two: a proper annotation protocol. We'll write a codebook with definitions and examples for our four error types. We'll measure inter-annotator agreement with Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha on an overlapping subset, with adjudication of disagreements.

Three: validate the L L M judge before scaling. We'll fix the judge model, version and prompt, and set an acceptance threshold, such as a minimum F one or kappa against the human labels. Only after the judge passes does it annotate the large set of one thousand to ten thousand responses.

Four: analysis. We'll define the hallucination rate precisely and compare the four strategies on answer accuracy, explanation errors and computational cost. We'll look for common hallucination patterns and test whether extra reasoning steps help or introduce new errors.

To summarize: even in a small pilot, correct answers often came with unsupported explanations, and extra reasoning steps did not reliably fix that. Our goal now is to measure this rigorously, with validated human annotation, a validated judge, and honest uncertainty estimates.
